The Title Process

From contract to closing, here's what happens.

Title work runs in the background of every real estate transaction. These are the six steps that move a property from contract to a clean, insured closing.

01

Order placed

Your agent, lender, or attorney submits the title order. We confirm receipt and assign a closer to the file, one point of contact from start to finish.

02

Title search

Our team searches public records going back 60 or more years, including deeds, mortgages, liens, judgments, easements, and tax records. Every encumbrance on the property gets identified.

03

Title examination

A licensed examiner reviews the search results for defects, gaps in the chain of title, and anything that could affect ownership. Issues are flagged before they become closing-table surprises.

04

Commitment issued

We issue a title commitment, a formal promise to insure. It lists the coverage terms and any remaining exceptions that need to be resolved before closing can proceed.

05

Curative work

Open liens, estate issues, judgment clearances, and gap problems are handled in-house by our curative team. We resolve them before you sit down at the table, not after.

06

Closing & policy issued

Documents are signed, funds disbursed, and instruments recorded with the county. Your owner's policy, and the lender's policy if applicable, is issued and on file.

General
What is title insurance?
Title insurance protects you against losses arising from defects in the title to your property. These defects can include unpaid liens from prior owners, errors in public records, undisclosed heirs with a claim to the property, forged documents, or problems with prior transfers. Unlike other insurance policies that protect against future events, title insurance protects against past events that were unknown at the time of purchase.
How is title insurance different from homeowner's insurance?
Homeowner's insurance protects your property against future events like fire, theft, and storm damage. Title insurance protects your ownership rights against past events, meaning claims and defects that already existed when you bought the property but weren't discovered until later. Both are important, but they cover entirely different risks.
Do I need title insurance if I'm paying cash?
Cash buyers are not required to purchase title insurance, but most attorneys and financial advisors strongly recommend it. Without a policy, there is nothing protecting your equity if a claim surfaces after closing, whether that's an undisclosed lien, a prior ownership dispute, or a forged document in the chain of title. A one-time premium for an owner's policy is modest relative to the protection it provides on what is likely your largest asset.
What is a title search?
A title search is a review of public records to trace the ownership history of a property and identify any encumbrances attached to it. We search deeds, mortgages, judgments, liens, tax records, and court filings, typically going back 60 or more years depending on the state. The search produces a report that forms the basis for the title commitment and, ultimately, your insurance policy.
Coverage
What does an owner's policy cover?
An owner's policy protects your right to own and use the property. It covers things like unpaid taxes or liens from prior owners, claims by heirs or other parties with an interest in the property, errors or omissions in deeds and legal descriptions, fraudulent documents in the chain of title, and boundary disputes that affect your title. Coverage is in effect for as long as you own the property, and in many cases extends to your heirs.
What's the difference between an owner's policy and a lender's policy?
A lender's policy (also called a loan policy) protects your mortgage lender's interest in the property, not yours. Most lenders require it as a condition of financing. An owner's policy protects you, the buyer. The two are separate policies covering separate interests. In most transactions, buyers purchase both. The lender's policy is required, and the owner's policy is strongly advisable.
Does title insurance cover issues that come up after closing?
Title insurance covers losses arising from defects that existed before or at the time of closing but were unknown, even if they don't surface until years later. It does not cover new liens or encumbrances you take on after you close (for example, a mortgage you take out yourself, or a contractor lien from work you had done). The protection is permanent for past title issues; it's not a forward-looking policy.
Cost
How much does title insurance cost in New Jersey and New York?
Premiums are set by the state insurance department in both New Jersey and New York, so every title company charges the same rate for the base policy. The cost varies based on the purchase price or loan amount. For an accurate number on your specific transaction, request a quote and we'll provide a full breakdown including all applicable fees.
Who pays for title insurance?
In New Jersey, it's customary for the buyer to pay for both the owner's policy and the lender's policy. In New York, the seller often pays for the owner's policy in residential transactions, while the buyer typically pays for the lender's policy. These customs can vary by transaction type and are subject to negotiation between the parties. Your attorney or agent can advise on what's standard for your specific deal.
Is title insurance a one-time cost?
Yes. You pay a one-time premium at closing, and coverage remains in force for as long as you own the property. There are no annual renewals, no recurring premiums. The lender's policy expires when the mortgage is paid off. The owner's policy stays active indefinitely, and in many cases passes to your heirs.
Process
How long does the title process take?
For a standard residential transaction, most title work is completed within five to ten business days of the order being placed. More complex files, including commercial deals, properties with a complicated ownership history, REOs, or properties requiring curative work, take longer depending on what surfaces in the search. We communicate actively throughout the process so you always know where the file stands.
What happens at the closing table?
At closing, all parties sign the transfer documents, loan documents (if applicable), and settlement statement. Funds are collected and disbursed, covering the purchase price, payoffs on any existing liens, taxes, and closing costs. Once everything is signed and funds are confirmed, documents are submitted to the county for recording. Your owner's policy is issued after the recording is confirmed, typically within a few weeks.
Do you handle closings in-house?
Yes. Our settlement department handles funds collected at closing directly, across both New Jersey and New York transactions. Having title and settlement under one roof eliminates the coordination delay that comes from working with multiple vendors, so the same team that knows your file is managing the table.
